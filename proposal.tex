\begin{abstract}
Earnings call transcripts and SEC financial filings are primary sources of financial information, yet it remains unclear whether models that better understand this language produce more reliable predictive signals. We investigate this question through three coordinated research components. The first systematically compares fine-tuning strategies, single-task, multi-task, and curriculum fine-tuning, across three model architecture families on three financial language understanding tasks. The second studies retrieval-augmented generation as an alternative to parametric fine-tuning, comparing sparse, dense, and hybrid retrieval strategies and evaluating whether retrieval can substitute for or complement domain adaptation. The third evaluates predictive transfer: whether language understanding improvements propagate to stock direction, earnings surprise, and volatility predictions, and whether this holds across market periods and industry sectors. Together, these components provide a thorough analysis of financial NLP methods, highlighting when strong language understanding actually translates into meaningful predictive performance.
\end{abstract}

\section{Problem Definition and Research Questions}

Earnings call transcripts and SEC annual reports contain financial information expressed in natural language, processed by market participants to form price expectations. Prior NLP work treats financial language understanding and market prediction as separate, leaving unclear whether a model that better extracts and reasons over financial text actually produces better downstream predictions. Our central research question is: \textit{does improved financial language understanding reliably transfer to improved market signal quality, and if so, under what conditions?}

This question is broken down into three sub-questions, each the focus of one research component. First, which fine-tuning strategies and model architectures produce the strongest financial language understanding, and does multi-task training help or hurt? Second, is retrieval-augmented generation a viable alternative to fine-tuning for financial QA, and how do retrieval quality and strategy affect end-to-end performance? Third, do language understanding improvements transfer uniformly across prediction targets, market conditions, and document types, or are the gains narrow and task-specific?

\section{Tasks, Data, and Evaluation}

\subsection{Language Understanding Tasks}

We evaluate three tasks that capture different aspects of financial language comprehension.

\textbf{Task 1: Numerical extraction.} A model identifies structured financial values (revenue, EPS, guidance figures) and their associated attributes from transcript text. Evaluation uses precision, recall, and F1 against manually verified annotations. We distinguish between exact match and partial credit to capture near-miss errors.

\textbf{Task 2: Evidence-grounded QA.} A model answers factual financial questions and cites the specific transcript spans that support its answer. Evaluation uses answer accuracy, citation exact match, citation partial match, and hallucinated number rate. The hallucination metric is computed by checking whether numerical values in generated answers appear in the source document.

\textbf{Task 3: Financial sentiment classification.} A model assigns positive, negative, or neutral sentiment to financial statements. This serves as a standard benchmark for financial language understanding, enabling comparison with established baselines. Evaluation uses macro-averaged F1.

\subsection{Downstream Prediction Tasks}

We evaluate predictive transfer on three targets derived from market data. \textbf{Stock direction} predicts whether a company's stock closes up or down in the trading window immediately following an earnings call. \textbf{Earnings surprise direction} predicts whether reported earnings per share beat, meet, or miss analyst consensus estimates; this tests whether transcript signals predict firm-specific news rather than just market sentiment. \textbf{Post-call volatility} predicts whether realized return volatility in the five trading days following a call falls in the top quartile of the distribution; this tests whether model uncertainty signals capture investor uncertainty, not just directional bias.

All prediction targets are treated as classification problems. For each target, a logistic regression classifier is trained on signals derived from model outputs (sentiment scores, extracted guidance values, uncertainty markers, and QA-derived features). Holding the prediction architecture constant across all language model conditions ensures that prediction differences are attributable to language understanding quality.

\subsection{Datasets}

The \textbf{lamini/earnings-calls-qa} corpus (approximately 860{,}000 transcript QA pairs) provides training data for Tasks 1 and 2. The \textbf{Bose345/sp500\_earnings\_transcripts} corpus provides transcripts for prediction experiments, covering tens of thousands of calls across S\&P 500 firms from 2010 to 2024. \textbf{Financial PhraseBank} \citep{Malo2014} provides 4{,}840 annotated financial sentences for Task 3. \textbf{FinanceBench} \citep{Islam2023} provides QA pairs over public company 10-K, 10-Q, and 8-K filings and serves as our cross-document-domain evaluation benchmark. \textbf{FinQA} \citep{Chen2021} and \textbf{ConvFinQA} \citep{Chen2022} provide additional numerical reasoning evaluation. Stock direction and volatility labels are computed from historical prices using \texttt{yfinance}. Earnings surprise labels are computed using analyst EPS consensus estimates from \texttt{yahooquery}. All data splits are chronological to prevent leakage.

\section{Component A: Fine-Tuning Strategies and Architecture Comparison}

\subsection{Model Families}

We evaluate three architecture families to determine whether fine-tuning benefits are architecture-specific or general across inductive biases.

\textbf{Encoder-only:} \texttt{ProsusAI/finbert} \citep{Araci2019}, a BERT-based model pretrained on financial corpora. We fine-tune with task-specific classification and span extraction heads. This model represents strong financial domain pretraining with a discriminative architecture.

\textbf{Encoder-decoder:} \texttt{google/flan-t5-base} \citep{Chung2022}, fine-tuned with task-prefixed input formatting. This allows direct evaluation of a generative seq2seq architecture on extraction and QA without the distributional mismatch of prompting-based generation.

\textbf{Decoder-only:} A publicly available instruction-tuned LLM (7B parameters) fine-tuned with LoRA \citep{Hu2022} at rank $r \in \{4, 16, 64\}$. We also evaluate the base model with prompting only to isolate fine-tuning effects.

\subsection{Fine-Tuning Regimes}

For each model family, we compare three training regimes:

\textbf{Single-task fine-tuning.} Each model is trained independently on each of the three understanding tasks. This provides baseline per-task performance and establishes the upper bound for task-specific optimization.

\textbf{Joint multi-task fine-tuning.} All three tasks are trained simultaneously with uniform or temperature-scaled task sampling. This tests whether financial language understanding tasks benefit from shared representations.

\textbf{Curriculum fine-tuning.} Tasks are presented in a fixed order designed to build from simpler (sentiment) to more complex (QA) linguistic demands. We test two orderings: sentiment $\rightarrow$ extraction $\rightarrow$ QA and extraction $\rightarrow$ sentiment $\rightarrow$ QA.

\subsection{Ablation Studies}

Within the decoder-only family, we ablate LoRA rank ($r \in \{4, 16, 64\}$), training data size (10\%, 25\%, 50\%, 100\% of available examples), and task ordering in curriculum fine-tuning. Within the encoder-only family, we ablate layer freezing (freeze bottom $k \in \{0, 6, 9\}$ transformer blocks). These ablations form a secondary contribution documenting the sensitivity of financial fine-tuning to engineering choices.

\subsection{Cross-Document-Domain Transfer}

All fine-tuned models are evaluated zero-shot on FinanceBench, which contains QA over SEC filings, without any SEC-specific training. This tests whether financial understanding trained on earnings transcripts generalizes to regulatory documents. We additionally fine-tune on a 50/50 mixed corpus of transcripts and SEC filings and compare zero-shot cross-domain performance before and after mixed training.

\section{Component B: Retrieval-Augmented Generation for Financial QA}

\subsection{Motivation}

Fine-tuning encodes domain knowledge in model weights, which is static after training. Retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) encodes knowledge in an external index that can be updated without retraining. For financial applications, where facts change quarterly and documents are long, RAG may be a more practical architecture. Component B investigates whether retrieval can substitute for or complement parametric fine-tuning on the QA task.

\subsection{Retrieval Strategies}

We implement and compare three retrieval strategies:

\textbf{Sparse retrieval (BM25).} A classical bag-of-words retrieval using BM25 scoring over transcript passage indices. This serves as a strong lexical baseline and is particularly relevant for retrieval of numerical entities, which have low paraphrase variation.

\textbf{Dense retrieval.} Passages are embedded using \texttt{sentence-transformers/all-MiniLM-L6-v2} \citep{Reimers2019} and indexed using FAISS. Query embeddings are computed at inference time, and top-$k$ passages are retrieved by cosine similarity.

\textbf{Hybrid retrieval.} BM25 and dense scores are combined via linear interpolation with a tunable mixture weight $\alpha$. We search over $\alpha \in \{0.2, 0.4, 0.6, 0.8\}$ on a development set.

For all strategies, we evaluate top-$k$ retrieval recall at $k \in \{1, 3, 5, 10\}$ against gold supporting spans. Retrieved passages are prepended as context for the reader model.

\subsection{Reader Models and RAG Conditions}

We evaluate four experimental conditions on the QA task:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item Base LLM with no retrieval and no fine-tuning (zero-shot prompting only)
    \item Base LLM with the best retrieval strategy (RAG only)
    \item Fine-tuned LLM with no retrieval (fine-tuning only)
    \item Fine-tuned LLM with the best retrieval strategy (RAG + fine-tuning)
\end{enumerate}

This 2$\times$2 factorial design isolates retrieval and fine-tuning effects and tests whether they are additive, redundant, or complementary.

\subsection{Retrieval Failure Analysis}

For cases where the system produces incorrect answers, we distinguish two failure modes: (1) retrieval failure, where the correct supporting passage was not retrieved; and (2) reader failure, where the correct passage was retrieved but the answer was still wrong. This decomposition identifies whether engineering effort should be directed toward better retrieval or better reading comprehension. We report failure mode breakdown by question type (numerical, factual, guidance-related) and document section.

\subsection{Long-Document Retrieval}

Earnings call transcripts can exceed 10{,}000 tokens. We study the effect of passage chunking granularity (sentence, 128-token, 256-token, 512-token windows) on retrieval recall and end-to-end QA accuracy, providing practical guidance for applying RAG to long financial documents.

\section{Component C: Predictive Transfer and Robustness Evaluation}

\subsection{Feature Extraction and Signal Construction}

For each model condition evaluated in Components A and B, we extract a fixed set of signals from transcripts: (1) overall sentiment score and section-level sentiment scores (prepared remarks, analyst Q\&A separately); (2) numerical guidance signals (extracted revenue/EPS guidance values, year-over-year change direction); (3) uncertainty markers (hedging language frequency, modal verb counts, extracted from QA and numerical extraction outputs); and (4) QA-derived confidence signals (answer length, citation coverage, hallucination rate as a proxy for model uncertainty). These signals are constructed identically across all model conditions, ensuring that prediction differences reflect language understanding rather than feature engineering.

\subsection{Prediction Models and Evaluation}

For each of the three prediction targets (stock direction, earnings surprise, volatility), we train logistic regression classifiers on the extracted signals. We evaluate using AUC-ROC, accuracy, balanced accuracy, and expected calibration error. Confidence intervals are computed via bootstrap resampling with $n = 1{,}000$ iterations. We test whether fine-tuned models produce significantly higher AUC than baselines using a paired permutation test.

To contextualize our findings, we compare against prior reported AUC values for earnings call prediction \citep{Qin2019}. We also compare predictions derived from Q\&A sections alone against prepared remarks alone, testing whether spontaneous analyst dialogue contains more predictive signal than scripted management statements.

\subsection{Temporal Robustness}

Using the chronological corpus, we partition test data into three market-period buckets: pre-COVID (2015--2019), COVID period (2020--2021), and post-COVID (2022--2024). Models are trained only on data prior to each evaluation period. We evaluate all model conditions on each period bucket and plot performance-over-time curves to identify degradation patterns. We additionally conduct vocabulary shift analysis to identify financial terminology that emerges in later periods (e.g., pandemic-era hedging language, supply chain terminology) and examine whether models trained on earlier periods handle novel vocabulary.

\subsection{Sector-Stratified and Firm-Size Stratified Analysis}

We stratify all prediction results by GICS sector (technology, healthcare, financials, industrials, and others) and by market capitalization tier (large cap vs.\ mid cap). This analysis tests whether language understanding improvements are concentrated in particular firm types---for example, whether fine-tuning benefits are larger for firms in sectors with more idiosyncratic language (healthcare regulatory language, technology forward guidance) than sectors with more standardized reporting.

\subsection{Structured Error Analysis}

We implement a structured error categorization pipeline across all tasks and prediction targets. For extraction, errors are labeled as: wrong number, wrong entity, hallucinated value, or missed mention. For QA, errors are labeled as: wrong span, unsupported citation, hallucination, or correct answer with wrong citation. For prediction, errors are analyzed along four dimensions: prediction target, sector, market cap tier, and model condition. Error profiles are compared across model families and fine-tuning regimes to identify whether architectural or training choices produce systematically different failure modes.

\section{Baselines}

We evaluate against four baselines of increasing linguistic sophistication: (1) majority class prediction; (2) Loughran-McDonald financial sentiment dictionary \citep{Loughran2011} applied to full transcript text; (3) zero-shot FinBERT; and (4) base instruction-tuned LLM without fine-tuning or retrieval. These baselines are evaluated identically to all fine-tuned conditions across all three prediction targets.

\section{Related Work}

Lexicon-based financial sentiment analysis \citep{Loughran2011} and domain-pretrained models such as FinBERT \citep{Araci2019} are the dominant approaches to financial NLP. Prior work shows earnings call language predicts market behavior \citep{Qin2019}, but transcripts are typically treated as flat documents. Numerical reasoning datasets such as FinQA \citep{Chen2021} and ConvFinQA \citep{Chen2022} evaluate financial comprehension but do not connect understanding to prediction. Large language model studies such as BloombergGPT \citep{Wu2023} examine financial pretraining at scale but do not systematically compare architecture families or fine-tuning strategies. Retrieval-augmented generation has been studied in open-domain settings \citep{Lewis2020}, but its relative utility compared to fine-tuning in financial document domains, and the effect of retrieval strategy choice, have not been characterized. Multi-task learning for NLP understanding tasks has been studied broadly \citep{Raffel2020} but not specifically for the relationship between financial task co-training and downstream predictive transfer.

\section{Progress Report}

\subsection{Infrastructure and Data Pipeline}

Since the proposal, we have implemented and validated the full end-to-end pipeline that Component C depends on. The pipeline is structured as a Python package under \texttt{src/} with separate modules for data loading, text preprocessing, feature extraction, classification, and evaluation. All experiments are run on the Ohio Supercomputer Center Pitzer cluster using a GPU-enabled Slurm job script. The environment is fully reproducible via a pinned dependency stack: PyTorch 2.3.1 built against CUDA 11.8 (required for V100/sm\_70 compatibility), Transformers 4.44.2, and supporting libraries.

The data pipeline loads transcripts from the \textbf{Bose345/sp500\_earnings\_transcripts} corpus and fetches stock price labels via \texttt{yfinance}, computing a binary direction label based on closing price movement over the three trading days following each call. Of the 500 most recent records sampled, 493 yielded valid price labels after excluding delisted or renamed tickers (PARA, BF.B, MMC, DFS, FI, IPG). All train/test splits are strictly chronological with no shuffling, yielding 394 training and 99 test examples. The label distribution in the sample is moderately imbalanced: 309 positive (price up) and 184 negative (price down), reflecting the upward drift of S\&P 500 constituents over the sampling window.

The text preprocessing module implements a regex-based section splitter that locates Q\&A boundaries using patterns such as \textit{``question-and-answer session''} and \textit{``open the floor to questions''}, separating each transcript into a prepared remarks segment and a Q\&A segment. This segmentation is central to the improved feature condition described below.

\subsection{Preliminary Baseline Results}

We have implemented the first two experimental conditions from Component C: a \textbf{baseline} using full-transcript FinBERT sentiment and an \textbf{improved} condition using section-separated sentiment scores. In both conditions, a logistic regression classifier with balanced class weights is trained on the extracted sentiment features. All hyperparameters are held constant across conditions.

\textbf{Baseline condition.} A single sentiment score is computed from the full transcript using \texttt{ProsusAI/finbert} \citep{Araci2019}, chunked into 150-word windows to handle transcripts exceeding the 512-token limit. The scalar score is defined as $P(\text{positive}) - P(\text{negative})$ and serves as the sole feature.

\textbf{Improved condition.} Three sentiment scores are computed independently from (1) the full transcript, (2) the prepared remarks segment, and (3) the analyst Q\&A segment. The three-dimensional feature vector tests whether section-level disaggregation provides complementary information relative to the aggregate signal.

\subsection{Analysis of Preliminary Results}

Several observations follow from these initial results. First, the baseline full-transcript AUC of 0.4883 is effectively at chance, confirming that aggregate sentiment over the full transcript provides no reliable directional signal. This is consistent with findings from prior work noting that management language in prepared remarks tends to be persistently positive regardless of underlying performance \citep{Qin2019}, and suggests that using the full transcript as a single undifferentiated unit conflates qualitatively different linguistic sources.

Second, the improved condition yields AUC 0.5122, a modest but positive improvement of $+0.0239$ over the baseline. While this improvement is small in absolute terms, it is directionally consistent with the hypothesis that section-level disaggregation recovers signal that the aggregate score masks. Notably, prepared remarks sentiment was computed nearly instantaneously during inference (less than one minute for 394 training examples), whereas full-transcript and Q\&A inference each took approximately 22 minutes on a V100 GPU. This timing asymmetry reflects that many transcripts in the corpus have short or absent prepared remarks segments under the current regex-based splitter---an issue we address in the next phase.

Third, the label imbalance ($\approx$62\%/38\%) deserves attention. The majority class baseline achieves 62.6\% accuracy by predicting all positives, substantially exceeding both model conditions. This reflects the upward bias in the sample, which draws from recent S\&P 500 calls; we will address this by expanding the sampling window to include earlier periods where market conditions were more varied, consistent with the temporal robustness analysis planned in Component C.

\subsection{Revised Plan for Remaining Work}

The preliminary experiments have validated the core infrastructure and identified three specific directions for the remaining work.

\textbf{Improving section segmentation.} The current regex splitter works well for transcripts that use standard transition language, but approximately 40\% of transcripts in the current sample yield an empty prepared remarks segment, contributing no differential signal in the improved condition. We will augment the splitter with additional patterns from the corpus and fall back to a heuristic positional split (first 60\% as prepared remarks) when no explicit marker is found.

\textbf{Expanding the feature set for Component C.} The preliminary experiments use only sentiment scores. The next implementation phase will add the numerical guidance signals and uncertainty markers described in the proposal: extracted EPS and revenue guidance direction (sign of year-over-year guidance change), modal verb density as a proxy for hedging language, and forward-looking statement frequency. These features do not require fine-tuned models and can be computed from the existing pipeline with straightforward extensions.

\textbf{Connecting Components A and B to Component C.} The preliminary results establish a quantitative lower bound using zero-shot FinBERT sentiment. The central research question is whether fine-tuning (Component A) or retrieval augmentation (Component B) improves these signals. We will implement single-task fine-tuning of FinBERT on Financial PhraseBank as the first Component A condition, replacing the zero-shot sentiment scores with fine-tuned probabilities and measuring the resulting change in AUC on the same prediction task. This creates a direct empirical link between language understanding improvement and predictive transfer---the core contribution of the project.

\textbf{Temporal stratification.} The current 80/20 chronological split is not yet partitioned by market period. We will implement the pre-COVID, COVID, and post-COVID evaluation buckets described in Section~5.3, using the full 33{,}362-transcript corpus rather than the 500-record sample. The larger corpus also reduces the label imbalance issue by including earlier periods with more varied return distributions.
